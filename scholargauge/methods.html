1. Novelty (gap) index, 0–100

Question it answers. Two ideas A and B are each studied in a context C (a field, or the whole of OpenAlex). Are papers that combine A and B rarer than the popularity of A and B would lead us to expect?

Counts

SymbolMeaning (OpenAlex search counts)
n(C)works matching the context C; all works in OpenAlex when no context is given
nA = n(A ∧ C)works matching idea A and the context
nB = n(B ∧ C)works matching idea B and the context
O = n(A ∧ B ∧ C)works matching both ideas and the context (observed)

Alternative spellings typed with ; are joined by OR inside each idea, e.g. ("drone" OR "UAV").

Expected count under independence

If a work in C mentioned A with probability pA = nA/n(C) and, independently, B with probability pB = nB/n(C), the number of works mentioning both would be Binomial(n(C), pApB) with mean

E=n(C)pApB = nAnBn(C) .

The ratio O/E is the classical observed-to-expected measure of co-occurrence: it is the "association strength" of co-word analysis[1],[20] and, for journal pairs, the "commonness" used to score the novelty of papers[6], commonness = Nijt Nt / (Nit Njt).

The index

Because pApB is small, the binomial count is very close to Poisson(E): by Le Cam's inequality[5] the total-variation distance is below n(C)(pApB)2 = E2/n(C). With X ~ Poisson(E),

G=100×P(X>O) =100 [1− ∑k=0O e−EEkk! ] =100(1−plow)

where plow = P(X ≤ O) is the exact one-sided p-value for H0: "A and B are combined at the independence rate" against H1: "they are combined less often". So G ≥ 95 means plow ≤ 0.05 and G ≥ 90 means plow ≤ 0.10.

How to read it

GReading shown by ScholarGauge
≥ 90studied much less than its parts suggest: a likely gap (with O = 0: "not done, although its parts are common")
60–90somewhat less studied than expected
40–60about as studied as its parts suggest
10–40somewhat more studied than expected
≤ 10crowded: studied more than its parts suggest

Counts are whole numbers, so when O equals E the index sits a little below 50 (O = E = 12 gives 42.4). When E < 1 and O = 0, G = 100(1 − e−E) < 64: a missing combination of two rare ideas is weak evidence of a gap, and ScholarGauge says so.

Worked example. Idea A "deep learning", idea B "Mughal painting", context "art history": n(C) = 400, nA = 40, nB = 40, O = 3.
E = 40 × 40 / 400 = 4.00; P(X ≤ 3) = e−4(1 + 4 + 8 + 10.667) = 0.4335; G = 100(1 − 0.4335) = 56.7 — about as expected.
Had no paper combined them (O = 0): G = 100(1 − e−4) = 98.2 — a clear gap. With E = 10 and O = 4, G = 97.1.

In "Has it been done?" for reliability designs

A = the chosen distribution, B = the chosen censoring scheme, each searched together with the chosen model; C = papers on the model (all of OpenAlex if no model is chosen); O = papers with all three. Each dictionary term is searched with its usual spellings joined by OR.

Where the idea comes from

Comparing how often two things occur together with how often they would by chance is the basis of co-word analysis (Callon et al., 1983)[1] and of normalised co-occurrence measures (van Eck and Waltman, 2009)[20]. Novelty studies apply it to pairs of journals cited together: Uzzi et al. (2013)[19] compare observed pair frequencies with randomised citation networks (z-scores), Lee, Walsh and Wang (2015)[6] use the observed-to-expected ratio ("commonness"), and Wang, Veugelers and Stephan (2017)[22] score first-time pairs. The notion of a gap between two literatures that are each well studied but rarely linked goes back to Swanson's "undiscovered public knowledge" (1986)[18].

What is ScholarGauge's own: turning the observed-versus-expected comparison for two ideas into a Poisson tail probability on a 0–100 scale, computed from OpenAlex search counts. It is not a published standard index, so in a paper describe it by its formula (see How to cite).

Limits. (i) Counts are OpenAlex full-text keyword matches (title, abstract and full text where available), so a paper that only mentions a term is counted; O and E share that error. (ii) Ideas are rarely independent: related ideas co-occur more than chance, so a low G does not mean "uninteresting", and a high G may reflect different vocabularies. (iii) The result depends on the context C you choose. (iv) A gap is a lead to read further, not proof of novelty; OpenAlex misses some regional journals and many abstracts.

2. Gaps on the topic map

The topic map reads the N most relevant OpenAlex papers for your query (100 or 200). For two keywords r and c among them, O = papers carrying both, nr and nc = papers carrying each, N = papers read that have keywords, and

E=nrncN

with the same index G. A cell is outlined as a gap when G ≥ 90 and E ≥ 1. The reliability view does the same for distribution × censoring scheme among papers whose design is recognised.

Many cells, many tests. 14 keywords give 91 pairs. Under independence each pair reaches G ≥ 90 with probability at most 0.10, so a few outlined cells can arise by chance; treat them as leads and check each with "Has it been done?", which uses the whole of OpenAlex rather than the papers read.

3. Variety (diversity) indices on a profile

The profile table measures how varied a researcher's papers are along one dimension at a time: OpenAlex topics, subfields, fields and domains; OpenAlex keywords; journals; co-authors; co-author countries; UN SDGs; and, when the reliability dictionary recognises them, distributions, censoring schemes, models and methods. The same indices are used in ecology for species and in scientometrics for disciplines[17].

Fractional counting

Paper j carries a set Lj of labels in the dimension (one primary topic, but possibly several keywords, co-authors or SDGs). Its weight 1 is split equally among its labels, so a paper with many labels does not count more than one with a single label[21]:

wi=∑j 1{i∈Lj}|Lj| , n=∑iwi , pi=win .

n (column "Papers") is the number of papers with at least one label in that dimension.

The columns

ColumnFormulaMeaningSource
KindsS = #{i : wi > 0}richness: number of different labels–
Effective kindsN1 = eH, H = −Σ pi ln pi Shannon entropy H turned into a number of kinds: the number of equally common labels that would give the same H Shannon (1948)[15]; Hill (1973)[3]; Jost (2006)[4]
SimpsonD = 1 − Σ pi2 Gini–Simpson index: chance that two papers drawn at random (with replacement) have different labels Simpson (1949)[16]
EvennessJ = H / ln S (S ≥ 2)Pielou evenness, 0–1; 1 when all labels are equally common Pielou (1966)[10]
Most commonarg max pithe commonest label and its share; ties are reported–

Richness, effective kinds and the inverse Simpson index are the Hill numbers of order 0, 1 and 2,

Dq =(∑ipiq)1/(1−q) : D0=S, D1=eH, D2=1/∑pi2

(1D is the limit as q → 1), so S ≥ eH ≥ 1/Σpi2 always, with equality only when all labels are equally common (Hill, 1973; Jost, 2006)[3],[4]. Effective numbers are easier to compare than entropies: doubling eH means twice as many equally common kinds.

Simpson's own estimator uses ni(ni − 1)/[n(n − 1)] to remove small-sample bias. With fractional counts that correction is not defined, so ScholarGauge uses the plug-in form; for a profile with few papers all indices are descriptive, not estimates of a population value.

Worked example (fractional counting). Three papers: paper 1 has keywords {a, b}, paper 2 has {a}, paper 3 has {c}. Weights: wa = ½ + 1 = 1.5, wb = 0.5, wc = 1; n = 3; p = (0.500, 0.167, 0.333).
S = 3; H = 1.0114, eH = 2.75; D = 1 − (0.25 + 0.028 + 0.111) = 0.61; J = 1.0114 / ln 3 = 0.92.
Two topics shared 2 : 1 by three papers give eH = 1.89, D = 0.44, J = 0.92.

4. Interdisciplinarity (Rao–Stirling diversity)

Variety ignores how different the labels are: two topics in statistics count as much as statistics and history. Rao–Stirling diversity weights each pair of topics by their distance dij:

Δ= ∑i∑j≠i dijpipj

pi = share of the profile's papers whose primary OpenAlex topic is i. This is Rao's quadratic entropy[14] and Stirling's general diversity heuristic Σi≠j dijα(pipj)β with α = β = 1[17], the form used to measure interdisciplinarity in bibliometrics[11],[13],[7].

Distance between two topics

ScholarGauge measures distance on OpenAlex's four-level classification (4 domains › 26 fields › 252 subfields › 4,516 topics)[9], in equal steps up the tree, as in taxonomic distinctness in ecology (Warwick and Clarke, 1995)[23]:

The two topics sharedij
the same subfield0.25
the same field, different subfields0.50
the same domain, different fields0.75
different domains1.00

Bibliometric studies often use 1 − cosine similarity of citation profiles instead (Porter and Rafols, 2009); the tree distance needs no citation data and is the same for every field, which keeps profiles from different disciplines comparable.

Range. Δ = 0 when all papers share one topic. Because dij ≤ 1, Δ ≤ 1 − Σpi2 (the Gini–Simpson index), with equality when all topics lie in different domains; so Δ < 1. Some authors sum over i < j only, which gives half this value; ScholarGauge sums over all ordered pairs, as in Rao (1982).

Worked example. Three papers: two in a statistics topic (field Mathematics) and one in a reliability-engineering topic (field Engineering); both fields belong to the domain Physical Sciences, so d = 0.75. p = (2/3, 1/3) and Δ = (2/3)(1/3)(0.75) + (1/3)(2/3)(0.75) = 0.33. Had the third paper been in a history topic (domain Social Sciences), d = 1 and Δ = 0.44 = the Gini–Simpson index.

5. Exploration

The share of a researcher's later papers that open a topic new to them. Papers are grouped by year; the topics of the first year form the starting set T. For each later year y, in order, every paper of year y whose primary topic is not in T counts as new; then the topics of year y are added to T.

Exploration= number of papers after the first year with a topic new to the authornumber of papers after the first year

Papers of the same year do not count each other as earlier work. 0% means every later paper stayed in topics already worked on; 100% means every later paper opened a new topic. It is a simple descriptive rate in the spirit of studies of topic switching (Zeng et al., 2019)[24] and of tradition versus innovation in research strategies (Foster, Rzhetsky and Evans, 2015)[2], not the measure of either paper.

Example. Topics by year: 2015 {T1}, 2023 {T1}, 2025 {T2}. Later papers: 2 (2023, 2025); new topic: 1 (T2 in 2025). Exploration = 1/2 = 50%.

6. Data behind the indices

  • Topics: OpenAlex assigns each work up to three topics with a deep-learning classifier that reads the title, abstract, citations and journal name; the highest-scoring one is the primary topic used here. About 12% of works get no topic because they lack text. The classification was developed with CWTS, Leiden University[9].
  • Keywords: OpenAlex keywords with score ≥ 0.4, lower-cased.
  • UN SDGs: since October 2026 OpenAlex tags goals with a classifier it trained and tested itself; a goal appears at a score of 0.4 or above. The score is a confidence, not a probability: OpenAlex reports tags scored 0.4–0.5 right about a quarter of the time, 0.5–0.8 about half the time, 0.8–0.9 about 7 in 10 and above 0.9 about 8 in 10[8]. ScholarGauge shows goals at ≥ 0.5 by default (Options). Scopus uses a different SDG mapping, so counts will not agree.
  • Counts for the novelty index: OpenAlex search results (quoted phrases joined with AND / OR); per_page=1 returns only the total.
  • Journal quartiles: SCImago Journal & Country Rank, official best quartile of each source for each year 1999–2025.
  • Indexed in Scopus: a paper counts when its journal, book series or conference was in the SCImago list for its year of publication. SCImago's lists are built from Scopus, so this counts papers in Scopus-covered sources; it can miss papers that Scopus covers outside those lists and cannot judge papers before 1999. Papers newer than the latest list use that list.
  • Indexed in Web of Science (optional, with your own free Clarivate key): each paper's DOI is looked up in the Web of Science Core Collection through Clarivate's Web of Science Starter API; papers without a DOI are not checked, and the API does not report which index (SCIE, SSCI, AHCI, ESCI…) a record belongs to.
  • OpenAlex itself is described by Priem, Piwowar and Orr (2022)[12].

7. How to cite these indices in a paper

Report the formula, the data source and the date of the counts, and cite the method papers. Suggested wording:

"Novelty was measured with the Poisson gap index G = 100·P(X > O), X ~ Poisson(E), where O is the number of OpenAlex works matching both ideas within the context and E = n(A∧C)·n(B∧C)/n(C) is the number expected under independence (cf. van Eck and Waltman, 2009; Lee, Walsh and Wang, 2015), computed with ScholarGauge 1.0.0 (KStats Lab, 2026) from OpenAlex counts retrieved on <date>."

"Research variety was summarised by the effective number of topics eH (Hill, 1973; Jost, 2006), the Gini–Simpson index (Simpson, 1949) and Pielou's evenness (Pielou, 1966) over OpenAlex topics with fractional counting, and interdisciplinarity by Rao–Stirling diversity (Rao, 1982; Stirling, 2007) with taxonomic distances on the OpenAlex topic hierarchy."
